Which pages need attention first?

Predicting search-visibility momentum from historical signals

Mary Joanna Cathaline S. Dulay · Built on the FlyRank ML Internship dataset

Abstract

Content teams cannot review every page, so which pages deserve attention first? We observe daily search-impression data for pages across many sites (FlyRank ML Internship dataset) and label each page-window as Growth, Recovery, Stable or Decline from the change in impressions per available day over the next 28 days (threshold 25%). A histogram gradient boosting classifier uses only the preceding 56 days of search and engagement signals, and is trained on earlier cutoffs and tested on a later, sealed month. On 86,910 held-out pages the model's macro F1 is 0.383, lower than the best simple baseline (momentum tree) by 0.034 macro F1 (95% interval -0.067 to -0.012). The ranked list is decision-support only: it is directional, not causal, and says nothing about how Google ranks pages.

Introduction

A site can hold thousands of pages and a content team has hours, not weeks. The decision this work supports is which pages to review first. Reviewing a page that would have been fine anyway wastes time; missing a page that was sliding costs visibility. The output is therefore a ranked queue with plain-language reason codes for a person to check, not an automatic action.

Methodology

Windows. For each cutoff date T: window A = days T−55 to T−28, window B = T−27 to T (history, used for features), window C = T+1 to T+28 (outcome, used only for the label).

Label. Using impressions per GSC-available day: Growth if C is at least 25% above B; Decline if at least 25% below; Recovery is Growth where B was itself at least 25% below A; otherwise Stable.

Features (13): impression level and change, last-7-day slope, CTR and its change, average position and change, volatility, share of active days, data availability, and GA4 coverage and engagement (left missing, not zero-filled, when GA4 was not reporting).

Baselines: always the majority class; “last window’s direction continues”; “last window’s move reverses”; a one-feature “recent slope” rule (a small decision tree using only the last-7-day trend); and a two-feature “momentum tree” (last-7-day trend and change versus the previous window). The last two are the demanding ones: they show how much of the model’s signal is simply recent momentum. The trees’ depth and class weighting were fixed on validation data only.

Validation: time-aware. Training cutoffs are spaced 28 days apart, so no training label window overlaps the next split; the test cutoff comes from the sealed final month. Primary metric: macro F1 (every class counts equally); accuracy is secondary. Uncertainty comes from resampling whole clients.

Leakage checks. (1) Features recomputed after physically deleting all rows after the cutoff were identical (max absolute difference 1.8e-15). (2) No single feature alone reached macro F1 above 0.400. (3) Client and page identifiers are never model inputs.

Limitations and honest framing

  • These are observed, directional associations in one panel of sites; they are not causal and do not show how Google ranks or refreshes pages.
  • Labels measure change in impressions, which also moves with seasonality, demand and algorithm updates outside the page’s control.
  • Window B appears in both the features and the label’s denominator, so part of the signal is regression to the mean.
  • Coverage is uneven: only pages with enough GSC-available days enter (86,910 of 409,326), and GA4 is missing for many page-days.
  • The outcome mix shifts sharply between cutoffs (Decline from 14.7% to 60.4% of pages), so a result from one month may not carry to another.
  • A few clients dominate: the two largest hold 44.2% of page-windows across 49 clients, so uncertainty is wide and results may not generalise to other sites.
  • One sealed outcome month: results may differ in other months and other sites.
  • Class balance is uneven (Decline 60.4%, Stable 25.3%, Growth 7.9%, Recovery 6.3%), so per-class scores matter more than accuracy.

Ranked recommendations (action playbook)

Queue logic: review_decline_risk = the top 5% of pages by (predicted decline probability × observed impressions); watch_recovery and support_growth = highest predicted Recovery / Growth probability. Each row carries reason codes derived from the page’s own recent signals. Checked on the sealed month:

Base rate of Decline across all test pages: 60.4%.

Reading the table honestly. Ranking pages by the weakest last-week trend alone found a higher share of true decliners (87.7%) than the model-based queue (79.2%). For decline risk alone, the simple rule was the better choice on this month.

The model’s distinct contribution is the other two slices: pages in watch_recovery actually recovered 39.4% of the time (all pages: 6.3%), and pages in support_growth actually grew 42.6% of the time (all pages: 7.9%).

Human review before acting: check for a known cause (seasonality, migration, tracking change); never auto-rewrite, merge or delete a page from this list. Stale triggers: re-evaluate when macro F1 on the newest labelled cutoff drops below the best baseline, or any key feature’s population stability index exceeds 0.25 (current largest: pos_B at 0.55).

Reproducibility

All code is in the project repository: notebooks w04_signal_audit, w05_model, w06_validation_audit, w07_action_playbook and capstone under work/notebooks/, shared code in work/src/. Fixed seed 42. Rebuilding the page-level tables can reorder rows, so a re-run may differ from the reported scores in the third decimal place.

Glossary

Every word with a dotted underline in this paper, and every chart bar, has a pop-up. They are all collected here. Type to search.

Terms

95% interval
A range that would contain the true difference about 95 times out of 100 if the study were repeated. If it includes zero, the difference is not reliable. In this paper: Worked out by resampling whole clients.
Accuracy
The share of all predictions that were exactly right. In this paper: Secondary, because one outcome is far more common than the others.
Average position
Where a page typically ranks in search results. 1 is the top. In this paper: Used as a signal, with its change from the previous window.
Baseline
A simple method used as a yardstick. A model is only useful if it beats a fair baseline. In this paper: Several baselines are compared, from always guessing the most common outcome to a small two-signal tree.
Causal
One thing making another happen. This study only sees patterns, so it cannot show causes. In this paper: The paper makes no causal claims.
Client
One website (brand) in the dataset. In this paper: Names are hidden. Only coded IDs exist.
CTR (click-through rate)
Clicks divided by impressions: of everyone who saw the page in search results, the share who clicked it. In this paper: One of the signals, together with how much it changed since the previous window.
Cutoff
The date a prediction is made. Only data up to that day is used as input. In this paper: Each page is scored at several cutoffs, 28 days apart.
Decision tree
A model that predicts by answering a short chain of yes/no questions about the inputs. In this paper: The simple baselines are small trees that can be read in full.
Decision-support
A tool that helps a person decide, rather than deciding for them. In this paper: The ranked list is a starting point for human review, not an automatic action.
Decline
An outcome class: impressions per day over the next 28 days came out at least 25% below the last 28. In this paper: The most common outcome in the final test month.
Directional
Shows which way things tend to move, without proving why or by exactly how much. In this paper: How this paper frames its findings.
F1
A score from 0 to 1 that combines precision and recall for one outcome. In this paper: Shown for each outcome separately: Growth, Recovery, Stable and Decline.
Features
The input signals a model looks at for each page. In this paper: Signals such as the last-7-day trend, CTR, position and how jumpy the traffic is.
GA4 (Google Analytics 4)
Google’s analytics tool that records what visitors do on a site, such as sessions and engagement. In this paper: Used for extra engagement signals. It is missing for many pages, so gaps are left blank, not filled with zeros.
Gradient boosting
A method that builds many small decision trees one after another, each fixing the mistakes of the ones before. In this paper: The main model. “Histogram” means it groups values into bins first, which makes it fast.
Growth
An outcome class: impressions per day over the next 28 days came out at least 25% above the last 28. In this paper: One of the four outcomes the model predicts.
GSC (Google Search Console)
Google’s free tool that reports how a site shows up in Google Search: how often it appeared, how often it was clicked, and where it ranked. In this paper: The source of the daily search numbers. Days when GSC data was missing are skipped, never counted as zero.
Hashed
Replaced by a one-way code, so the original name cannot be recovered. In this paper: Client and page identifiers are hashed.
Held-out
Kept out of training and used only for testing. In this paper: The pages scored in the final test month.
Impressions
How many times a page appeared in search results, whether or not anyone clicked. In this paper: The paper’s measure of visibility. Labels come from the change in impressions per day.
Label
The correct answer a model learns to predict. In this paper: Growth, Recovery, Stable or Decline, worked out from the change in impressions over the next 28 days.
Leakage
When information from the future sneaks into a model’s inputs, making it look better than it would in real use. In this paper: Checked by deleting every later row and rebuilding the signals. They came out identical.
Lift
How many times more often something happens in a chosen group than overall. In this paper: A lift of 1.3× means flagged pages declined 1.3 times as often as pages overall.
Macro F1
A score from 0 to 1 for a classifier. For each outcome it balances precision (how often a prediction is right) and recall (how many real cases are found), then averages the outcomes with equal weight. In this paper: The paper’s main score. Every outcome counts equally, so a rare one like Recovery matters as much as a common one.
Majority rule
A baseline that always predicts the most common outcome in the training data. It is the floor any real method must beat. In this paper: Scores low on macro F1 because it never predicts the three rarer outcomes.
Mean-reversion rule
A baseline that predicts the opposite of last window’s move: a fall bounces back and a rise fades. In this paper: The mirror image of the persistence rule.
Momentum tree
A small decision tree using two signals: the last-7-day trend and the change from the previous 28 days. In this paper: The strongest simple baseline in this study.
Permutation importance
Shuffle one signal and see how much the score drops. A bigger drop means the model leans on that signal more. In this paper: Shows association, not proof of cause.
Persistence rule
A baseline that assumes last window’s direction continues: pages that rose keep rising and pages that fell keep falling. In this paper: It never predicts Recovery, since Recovery means a reversal.
Population stability index (PSI)
A measure of how much a signal’s distribution has shifted between two periods. About 0.1 is small and above 0.25 is large. In this paper: A re-fit trigger: if a key signal passes 0.25, the model should be re-checked.
Random split
Shuffling rows into training and test sets without looking at dates. The same page can land on both sides, which flatters the score. In this paper: Shown only to measure how much a careless test would overstate the result.
Reason codes
Short plain labels, like falling_impressions, that say why a page was flagged. In this paper: Worked out from each page’s own recent signals so a person can check them.
Recent-slope rule
A tiny decision tree that uses one signal only: the page’s last-7-day trend compared with its 28-day average. In this paper: A demanding baseline. It shows how much of the model’s skill is just recent momentum.
Recovery
An outcome class: a page that fell in the previous window and then rose by at least 25%. In this paper: The rarest and hardest class to predict.
Regression to the mean
Pages with unusually high or low traffic tend to drift back toward average next time, even if nothing is done. In this paper: Part of the signal may be this, because window B sits in both the inputs and the label.
Resampling
Re-drawing random samples from the data many times to see how much a result would wobble. In this paper: Whole clients are redrawn together, because pages on one site are not independent.
review_decline_risk
A queue slice: pages with the highest predicted chance of declining, weighted by how much traffic they carry. In this paper: The top 5% of pages by that score.
Sealed test
Data kept out of sight and used for one final check, after every choice is frozen, so the score is not flattered by tuning. In this paper: The final month was sealed and scored once.
Seed
A starting number for random choices, so the same run gives the same result. In this paper: Fixed, so the experiments can be repeated.
Share of test pages
The percentage of final-test pages whose real outcome fell in this class. In this paper: Shows how lopsided the classes are.
Stable
An outcome class: impressions per day moved by less than 25% either way. In this paper: The model finds this class the hardest to recognise.
support_growth
A queue slice: pages predicted to keep growing. In this paper: Candidates to protect and support.
Time-aware split
Training on earlier dates and testing on later ones, the way the model would really be used. In this paper: Training cutoffs are spaced 28 days apart, so no outcome window overlaps the next split.
Unseen clients
Test pages from sites that were completely absent from training. In this paper: A check that the model does not just memorise particular sites.
Volatility
How much a page’s daily traffic jumps around. In this paper: One of the signals.
watch_recovery
A queue slice: pages predicted to bounce back after a fall. In this paper: Look, but do not rewrite a page that is already recovering.
Window
A fixed stretch of days used to build signals or outcomes. In this paper: A and B are 28 days of history. C is the next 28 days, whose result becomes the label.
